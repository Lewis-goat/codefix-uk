Samsung ranges and wall ovens — the NE and NX series and their NV and NZ siblings — speak two error dialects. Two-part codes such as E-08 and E-27 report on the oven's heating parts, and short codes such as SE and tE report on the control panel itself. **SE is the panel code you are most likely to live with**: the board believes a key on the touchpad is stuck or shorted. It belongs to a small family — C-d0 (stuck key detected), tE (touch communication) and E-55 (display communication) — and almost every member points at the same part: the touchpad membrane. You can see the whole group in the [Samsung oven code index](https://uk.codefixcoffee.com/samsung-oven-error-codes/).

## What SE actually reports

Behind the front panel is a thin printed switch sheet. Press "Bake" and two contacts touch; release and they separate. [SE](https://uk.codefixcoffee.com/samsung/range-wall-oven/se/) means the control board is reading one of those switches as held closed when nobody is touching it. Three things produce that reading:

- **Moisture or grease under the membrane** bridges the contacts, so the board sees a press that never ends. Water from a recent cleaning is the everyday trigger, and the same moisture sits behind most tE codes.
- **A genuinely stuck key**, where debris or a worn switch dome holds one button down.
- **A failed membrane**, whose printed traces short internally — or, on older ranges, a dirty ribbon-cable connection between the membrane and the control board.

One thing you cannot do is read a log. Samsung ovens have no user-accessible error log; the code stays on the display until the cause is fixed or you cut power at the breaker for 3 minutes. If it returns after that reset, treat it as real rather than re-resetting and hoping.

## The fix sequence, in order

1. Cut power at the breaker for 3 minutes and restore it.
2. Press each key once — including the one that reads as stuck — and wipe the panel with a barely damp cloth. If the panel was cleaned recently, let it dry fully before restoring power.
3. If SE comes back, remove the back of the control panel with the power off and reseat the membrane ribbon on the control board.
4. If it still comes back, replace the membrane or touch panel.

A code that clears after step 2 was moisture, and you are done. A code that survives a dry panel and a reseated ribbon is a component, not a cleaning problem.

## Cleaning the panel properly

- Use a barely damp microfibre cloth and wring it out first; liquid that runs into the seams around the keys is what starts these faults.
- Never spray cleaner directly onto the panel — spray the cloth instead.
- Let the panel dry completely before restoring power; moisture that trips SE or tE usually clears once the panel is genuinely dry.
- Deal with greasy films promptly rather than letting them build up, since grease under the membrane behaves exactly like water.

## The rest of the touch family

C-d0 is the same fault as SE on many models: press the key that shows as stuck and all the others once, cut power for 3 minutes, and replace the membrane if it persists. [tE](https://uk.codefixcoffee.com/samsung/range-wall-oven/te/) is one step further along — the touch panel is not responding to the board at all. Moisture after cleaning is again the everyday cause; then the ribbon; then the panel. E-55 is a display or touch panel communication error: a loose ribbon, a wet panel, or a failed panel or board. The ladder is the same for every code in this family — dry it, reseat it, replace it — and the parts get more expensive as you climb.

## When replacing the membrane is worth it

A replacement membrane costs €40 to €90 where the range uses a separate membrane, and €100 to €200 where the keys are part of a sealed glass touch panel. That is cheap for the fault it fixes: SE is a low-severity code with a moderate DIY rating, and the replacement is worth doing on almost any range still in service. Cut power at the breaker before opening the control panel — mains voltage sits behind it — and follow the ribbon from the membrane to the board so the new one goes back the same way.

The alternative is a technician's visit at roughly €120 to €250 for diagnosis plus the part, which on a membrane-only fault can exceed the cost of the part itself. If you are comfortable with a screwdriver, the membrane swap is the economical route. If a brand-new membrane does not clear the code, stop spending there and get a quote: the fault has moved to the board, and the [board-level codes in the Samsung index](https://uk.codefixcoffee.com/samsung-oven-error-codes/) are a different decision on an older range.
