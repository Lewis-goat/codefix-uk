title: "Is it worth repairing? The honest math"
description: "Repair math for error codes: part cost plus labour versus machine class, honest thresholds by tier, and when a refurb unit beats the quote."
---

An error code on the display is only half the diagnosis. The other half is arithmetic: is this machine worth the fix, or is today the day it becomes a source of spare parts? The honest answer depends on three things — the cost of the part, who fits it, and the class of machine it is going into. None of them involve sentimentality about the machine you bought years ago.

## Two numbers before anything else

Start with the replacement value, not the purchase price. What you paid three years ago is irrelevant; what the same class of machine costs today is the number that matters. Then find the realistic repair cost: the part, plus labour if you are not fitting it yourself, plus a little slack for the second fault that occasionally hides behind the first.

A common rule of thumb says a repair is worth making while it stays under half of replacement cost. That is a sensible default, but the threshold genuinely moves with the tier of machine — in both directions.

## What the most common codes actually cost

The encouraging pattern in repair tables is that the codes people meet most often sit at the cheap end of the parts list.

- **Jura Error 8** is a brew-group cycle failure, and most cases clear with a cleaning tablet at €15 to €25 a box. Even the worse outcomes are moderate: a brew group runs €80 to €150, a drive motor €40 to €70. The [Error 8 reference](https://uk.codefixcoffee.com/jura/automatic-machines/error-8/) calls it the code a tablet usually fixes, and the maths agree.
- **Jura Error 2**, the single most common Jura fault, has a benign form that costs nothing: a machine kept below about 10 °C simply locks the heater out until it warms up. The real fault — a thermoblock sensor or thermal-fuse cords — means a genuine sensor at €25 to €40 plus a cord set at €15 to €30. The [Error 2 page](https://uk.codefixcoffee.com/jura/automatic-machines/error-2/) walks both paths.
- **GE dryer E4** is a blown thermal fuse at €5 to €15, or a high-limit thermostat at €10 to €25 — on a [dryer that still runs](https://uk.codefixcoffee.com/ge/dryer/e4/), about as cheap as repairs get. The catch: you must also fix the airflow restriction that blew the fuse, or the new one follows it.
- **Samsung E-27** is an oven temperature sensor reading open. The part is €20 to €40 and the [fix is rated easy](https://uk.codefixcoffee.com/samsung/range-wall-oven/e-27/): two screws inside the cavity, a plug, done.
- **Philips and Saeco Error 05** is air in the water circuit, and priming a freshly fitted filter costs [nothing at all](https://uk.codefixcoffee.com/philips-saeco/espresso-machines/error-05/).

Against numbers like these, a working machine is almost never worth scrapping.

## Labour is what changes the arithmetic

Who turns the screwdriver matters more than the part does. Fitted by you, the repairs above cost their part prices. Fitted by a workshop, the calculus shifts:

- Out-of-warranty manufacturer service on a super-automatic coffee machine typically runs €250 to €500 including return shipping; independent espresso repairers are usually cheaper for a single-part job.
- A home visit from an appliance technician for a dryer or range runs €120 to €250 for diagnosis plus the part.

A €30 sensor becomes a €180 repair the moment it crosses a workshop counter. That is not a rip-off — it is skilled time, a bench test and a warranty on the work — but it is the number that decides the question.

## Thresholds by machine tier

1. **Entry bean-to-cup, roughly €300 to €700 new.** Tighten the 50 percent rule toward 40. A €250 quote against a €500 machine is a poor trade unless the machine is nearly new.
2. **Mid-range machines, €700 to €1500.** The standard rule holds: repair while the total stays under half of replacement cost.
3. **Premium machines — Jura S, Z and GIGA territory, €1500 and up.** Loosen the threshold toward 60 or 70 percent. Parts supply is good, the build quality rewards repair, and replacement is genuinely expensive.
4. **Dryers, ovens and other white goods.** Sensor, fuse and switch faults are almost always worth fixing on any age of appliance; control boards at €100 to €200 are the case-by-case part.

## When a refurbished unit beats the quote

Sometimes the honest answer is to stop. Three situations point that way:

- The quote lands at or above the price of a factory-refurbished unit — which arrives with a warranty, and your repair may not.
- An entry-tier machine around the ten-year mark needs a major assembly. Practitioner guidance on the [Jura codes](https://uk.codefixcoffee.com/jura/) says exactly this: on a ten-year-old entry E, ENA or Impressa, compare the quote with a refurbished unit; on high-end Z and GIGA machines the same repair is worth making.
- Faults are cascading — a sensor, then a valve, then a board inside a year. Machines communicate old age through their error codes.

Run the numbers before you run the emotions. Name the part, price it, price the labour, and hold both against today's replacement cost. Most codes survive that maths comfortably. The rest were telling you something.
