Error 2 is the most common code on Jura automatic machines — the E, ENA, S, J, Z and GIGA ranges all share the same numbered set — and it has two very different faces. Either the coffee thermoblock's temperature-sensor circuit has gone open circuit, or the machine is simply too cold to heat. In winter the second one catches out a surprising number of owners who have done nothing wrong, and it is the cause the error message itself never hints at. The full reference is on the Jura Error 2 page; this article is about the cold half of the story.

## What the machine is actually telling you

Jura's Errors 1 to 5 all concern the thermoblock heaters and their sensors. When the electronics see a temperature reading they cannot reconcile with the heating they commanded, they shut the heater down and refuse to retry — a protective lockout, not necessarily a failure. Below roughly 10 °C, a cold thermoblock sits far outside the range the board expects, and the machine treats that exactly as it would a faulty sensor. Nothing is broken. The machine is cold.

The scenario is classic: a machine delivered in winter after a ride in an unheated van, a Jura kept in a cold kitchen, conservatory, garage office or holiday home, or one freshly unboxed and started straight away. The pattern is always the same — it worked fine before, Error 2 appears at the first start-up, and nothing you press clears it.

Coffee machines in general dislike starting cold. Philips and Saeco machines have an equivalent code: Error 11 or 19 means the machine needs to adjust to room temperature after cold transport — see Philips Error 11 or 19 for that brand's version.

## The warming protocol

Run this before concluding anything is broken. It costs nothing and it is always the first thing to try on a cold machine.

- Move the machine to a heated room and give it time — several hours to reach true room temperature, not just to feel less cold on the outside. A chassis that seems fine can still hold a thermoblock well below 10 °C.

- To speed things up, run a hair dryer on its lowest setting into the water-tank cavity for about five minutes, or fill the tank with warm — not hot — tap water.

- Restart the machine.

- If Error 2 clears after warming, nothing has failed. Keep the machine somewhere warmer and the code will not return.

Two cautions. Warm water means warm, not hot: the tank, its valves and seals are plastic. And do not aim concentrated heat at the machine's body or electronics — the goal is to take the chill off the thermoblock, not to cook the wiring around it.

## When it really is the NTC sensor or the thermal-fuse cords

If the machine has genuinely warmed — it has stood for hours in a heated room — and Error 2 still appears, the benign explanation is gone and the sensor circuit is open. Inside a Jura that means one of two findings:

- The NTC temperature sensor on the coffee thermoblock has failed, or its plug is no longer making contact. The sibling code is Jura Error 1, the coffee-thermoblock sensor fault proper; the two faults share parts and symptoms.

- The two thermal-fuse cords that protect the thermoblock have gone open circuit — they blow after an overheat, or simply with age. A blown fuse cord reads open on a multimeter.

The repair is usually worthwhile. A genuine Jura NTC sensor runs about €25 to €40 and a thermal-fuse cord set €15 to €30; technicians normally replace both together because the labour is the same. Even a full thermoblock, at €90 to €180, can still make sense on the higher machines. If your Jura was warm all along, this — not the weather — is your Error 2.

## The warning sign after the repair

One trap deserves its own paragraph. A thermal-fuse cord that has blown once will blow again if the power board is latching the heater on. If a newly fitted fuse cord fails within days, stop replacing fuses: the power board is the fault. A board typically costs €120 to €250 plus labour, which on an older machine belongs in a quote-versus-replacement conversation rather than a parts order.

## Safety, and knowing your limits

Opening a Jura is not like opening a kettle. The case is held with oval-head Torx-Plus security screws, and the thermoblocks carry mains voltage. If you do not have the driver, a meter, and comfort working around mains, this stage is a bench repair. The warming protocol is the user-level half of the Error 2 story; the sensor and fuse cords are the workshop half.

The economics stay friendly either way. Warming costs nothing, and the realistic parts bill for the sensor-and-fuse-cord fix sits under about €50. For the rest of the brand's code set — the valve, brew-group and heater codes across every model range — start from the Jura overview.
