"De'Longhi General Alarm: what it actually means"

2026-10-12

Of everything a De'Longhi super-automatic can put on its display, "General Alarm" sounds the most ominous and tells you the least. That is not an accident. De'Longhi machines mostly show words, not codes: a Magnifica, Dinamica or PrimaDonna tells you the kind of problem it thinks it has rather than quoting a service number at you. Behind the scenes, newer models also log a numeric code — 1101, 1454, 2257 and so on — which the service department reads when the machine comes in. Both layers, the words and the hidden numbers, are listed on each page of our De'Longhi section, starting with the General Alarm (code 1101 / 1512) reference. Here is what the message actually means, and what to do first.

A catch-all, not a diagnosis

General Alarm is the control board saying: I detected a fault, and I cannot isolate which component caused it. On machines that show numbers, it is logged as code 1101 or 1512. The hidden numeric log can be more specific elsewhere — 1454, for instance, is the grinder-stall code — but General Alarm is deliberately broad, and the display stays deliberately vague.

In practice, on ECAM and ESAM machines, it is the infuser — the removable brew unit — nine times out of ten. The infuser is stuck, dirty, or out of position after cleaning, or coffee dust has settled on the small optical sensor that reads its position. The next most common cause is scale in the water circuit making the pump labour, which the board also reports as a general fault rather than a precise one.

The good news buried in the vagueness: almost every General Alarm is a free fix that takes ten minutes.

The ten-minute routine

  1. Switch off at the switch on the back, wait 30 seconds, and switch on again. A one-off glitch clears here.
  2. Open the service door, press the two red buttons and pull out the infuser.
  3. Rinse it under running water — no soap — work the piston by hand, and let it dry.
  4. Look into the cavity the infuser came from. There is a small sensor window in there; wipe coffee dust off it with a dry cloth.
  5. Refit the infuser until it clicks, with its lever fully down, then close the door and restart.

If the alarm comes back

Run a descale cycle. A scaled circuit can trigger the alarm even when the infuser is spotless, and on machines older than a year descaling is the fix more often than not. If the infuser will not move by hand, or you can see a cracked seal on it, stop adjusting and replace it — the assembly is kit 7313251451 or 7313251441 depending on model, and swapping it is a five-minute job.

Why De'Longhi shows words, not codes

The philosophy behind the display is that words prompt an action and numbers prompt a search. You cannot act on "1101" without a service table, but "General Alarm" at least tells you to stop and look. The hidden numeric layer exists for the technician with the table, which is why we list both on every page rather than making you choose.

The other De'Longhi messages follow the same design, each naming the action it wants from you. Water Circuit Empty / Fill Circuit appears when air has got into the pump and it cannot prime. Insert Grounds Container appears when the contacts behind the container misread wet grounds or sludge as a missing container. None of these is a precise component diagnosis either — they are instructions.

If you want to see the opposite philosophy, look at Nespresso: Vertuo machines report most faults as blinking-light patterns instead of words, and there is a light-pattern decoder for exactly that reason. De'Longhi's whole approach, and every message in the family, is covered on the De'Longhi hub.

What it costs

  • Nothing, in most cases: the fix is a rinse, a wipe of the sensor window and a restart.
  • Descaler, if scale is the trigger: around €10.
  • A replacement infuser assembly, if yours is cracked or seized: €35 to €60.
  • Out-of-warranty manufacturer service on a super-automatic: typically €250 to €500 including return shipping; independent espresso repairers are usually cheaper for a single-part fix.

So when General Alarm appears, resist the urge to read it as a death sentence. It is the machine admitting it does not know exactly what went wrong — and nine times out of ten, a clean infuser and a wiped sensor are the whole answer. Start with the routine above, work it in order, and only think about parts or service once the alarm has survived a rinse and a descale.
